# Results for discussion

I ask two questions about the Texas and North Carolina maps: what electoral outcomes their boundaries produce when votes stay fixed, and how they group people geographically. I compare each enacted map with the same reference ensemble.

Using the modeled 2024 presidential vote, Texas scores 8 Democratic-majority districts under the enacted map, compared with a reference mean of 12.86. There are 76 states at or below that score among 556,000 retained observations.
Using the modeled 2024 presidential vote, NC scores 3 Democratic-majority districts under the enacted map, compared with a reference mean of 5.32. There are 1,020 states at or below that score among 135,000 retained observations.

Texas scores 8 Democratic-majority districts in all 9 existing statewide contests, while their reference means range from 12.08 to 17.20 on the saved checkpoints.

NC's comparison changes with the election. Under the 2024 governor vote allocation, its 2023 and 2025 maps score 12 and 13 Democratic-majority districts, compared with a reference checkpoint mean of 11.22. Each comparison holds one election's vote allocation fixed.

The geographic assessment depends on the property measured. Earlier calculations found shorter, more compact enacted boundaries alongside weaker population clustering. I now retain mean distance and its variation separately, since dividing SD by mean can remove a distance-scale difference.

The correlation figures show how geographic and electoral scores vary together among the saved maps, with every election retained. They do not turn the two questions into one overall verdict.
Texas's mean distance exceeds every saved checkpoint, while its CV of 1.245 falls within the reference range of 1.129 to 1.598. The ratio describes relative variation rather than distance scale.

## Limitations

The new geographic results remain preliminary because some convergence screens fail. The reference ensemble and modeled votes have documented constraints and input limitations. These comparisons do not establish partisan intent, legal compliance or a calibrated probability of gerrymandering.
