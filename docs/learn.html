Understanding redistricting

You don't need a math degree to understand gerrymandering. Here's what's happening, why it matters, and where to dig deeper.

Every ten years after the census, states redraw the boundaries of their congressional and legislative districts. When those lines are drawn fairly, districts reflect where people actually live. When they're drawn to give one party an advantage, that's gerrymandering.

There are two main tactics. Packing concentrates the opposing party's voters into a few districts so they win those seats by huge margins but waste most of their votes. Cracking spreads them across many districts so they never have quite enough to win anywhere. Either way, the outcome gets decided by the person drawing the map, not by the voters.

This isn't just a left or right problem. Both parties have done it in states they control. The question this project asks is simple: does a given map look like something a fair process could have produced, or does it look like the result was predetermined?

Key court cases

CaseStateWhat happened
LULAC v. Abbott (2025, ongoing)TXChallenge to 2025 mid-decade map. District court found racial gerrymandering. Supreme Court reversed on procedure. Pending.
League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth (2018)PACourt struck down congressional map using ensemble analysis. Landmark case for this methodology.
Rucho v. Common Cause (2019)NCSupreme Court ruled federal courts can't hear partisan gerrymandering claims. Pushed cases to state courts.
Allen v. Milligan (2023)ALCourt ruled Alabama's map violated the Voting Rights Act. Required an additional majority-minority district.
